# Waystone — Journey design principles

**Status:** enduring pacing guidance, reviewed 2026-09-22. It is a design standard for
future Expedition and Campaign sequencing, not a claim that the current generator already
implements the complete waveform. See `docs/current-state.md` for the present boundary.

## Purpose

This document describes how Waystone should turn good individual situations into a satisfying journey. It applies to both Expedition and Campaign, while recognising that the two modes create pacing in different ways:

- **Expedition** controls a forward-directed sequence and can author a strong run-wide waveform.
- **Campaign** places situations in a persistent world and lets the player influence pacing through route choice, retreat, rest, and return travel.

This complements `expedition-design-principles.md`, which remains the fuller reference for Expedition's run grammar, and `expedition-encounter-redesign.md`, which addresses encounter generation. This document is concerned with what each beat does **at this point in the journey**.

The central principle is:

> Every situation should be worthwhile, but not every situation should try to be the climax.

A journey made entirely from climactic encounters becomes flat. It lacks contrast, anticipation, resolution, and room for the player to understand what has changed. Quality and intensity are separate qualities.

---

## 1. What an effective journey does

An effective journey repeatedly:

1. establishes a readable situation;
2. develops or recombines what the player understands;
3. increases pressure or commitment;
4. introduces a contrast, complication, or twist;
5. reaches a local peak;
6. resolves that peak completely;
7. changes the kind of demand placed on the player;
8. builds toward a different or greater peak.

This is not simply an alternation of hard and easy fights. It is a change in **what the player is doing and feeling**. Combat intensity may fall while navigation, anticipation, scarcity, discovery, or build commitment remains meaningful.

The desired rhythm is therefore:

> Build pressure, sustain it, resolve it, change the demand, and then build again.

There is no universal proven ratio of combat to respite or peaks to valleys. Successful games use different proportions. The reusable pattern is not a fixed count but a controlled relationship among escalation, contrast, completion, and consequence.

---

## 2. Three nested pacing curves

Journey pacing operates at three scales at once.

### 2.1 The encounter

**Read → commit → complication → peak → resolution**

The player first identifies the problem, then makes a consequential commitment. The encounter complicates that initial understanding, reaches its most demanding state, and ends cleanly enough for the result to register.

Not every encounter needs all five phases. A brief ambush or mastery encounter may be valuable precisely because it is concise. The sequence is a diagnostic model, not a mandatory script.

### 2.2 The locale or route segment

**Orient → develop → contrast → twist → local climax → release**

A locale establishes its geography and tactical language, develops that language through several situations, interrupts repetition, then pays off its identity in a memorable local peak. The release may be a shortcut, safe overlook, discovery, reward, NPC, supply opportunity, route choice, or moment of uneasy quiet.

### 2.3 The complete journey

**Establish → broaden → disrupt → ordeal → recontextualise → culminate**

The full journey begins with a comprehensible promise, broadens its possibility space, breaks an established expectation, tests what the player has built and learned, gives earlier material new meaning, and culminates in a final demand that feels earned.

These curves should rhyme without becoming mechanically predictable. If every third room is a respite and every locale twists at the same position, pacing becomes a visible formula rather than a lived journey.

---

## 3. Pacing has several dimensions

Difficulty is only one component of intensity. A situation can demand more or less of the player along several independent dimensions:

- **Immediate threat:** how close the player is to taking damage or losing the run.
- **Attention:** how many actors, areas, timings, or warnings must be monitored.
- **Novelty:** how much is not yet understood.
- **Commitment:** how costly it is to reverse a route, action, build, or resource decision.
- **Attrition:** how much health, healing, Energy/recovery capacity, consumables, or future flexibility is at stake.
- **Navigation:** how uncertain the route or spatial relationship is.
- **Information:** how much scouting, inference, memory, or incomplete knowledge matters.
- **Anticipation:** how strongly the game implies that something important is approaching.
- **Emotional consequence:** how much the current situation threatens or resolves something the player values.

Good pacing changes this vector rather than merely raising or lowering a single difficulty number.

A quiet crossroads can have low immediate threat but high route commitment. A merchant can have no combat threat but high build commitment. A return path can have low novelty and high mastery. A boss approach can be mechanically sparse but emotionally intense.

### Frozen time does not remove the need for relief

Waystone freezes whenever the player is ready. This removes real-time reaction fatigue, but it does not remove:

- decision fatigue;
- strategic uncertainty;
- attrition pressure;
- emotional tension;
- the burden of monitoring several threats;
- the need to absorb the meaning of a victory or discovery.

Thinking time is not the same as respite. A frozen battlefield can remain an unresolved crisis. Conversely, a short but safe choice can provide meaningful release even if little world time passes.

For this reason, pacing should be measured primarily through commitments, decisions, changes of state, and simulation events—not real-world seconds alone.

---

## 4. Respite is a designed beat

Respite is not simply the absence of enemies. It has a job.

A good respite beat may:

- finish the previous crisis mechanically and emotionally;
- let the player inspect health, healing, Energy/recovery capacity, consumables, and build state;
- present an equipment, upgrade, or route choice;
- reveal how landmarks and paths relate;
- preview the next enemy or environmental language at low risk;
- allow a familiar threat to demonstrate the player's growing mastery;
- deliver an NPC, story, environmental detail, or mystery;
- tempt the player with optional danger;
- make the consequence of the previous victory visible;
- create silence and anticipation before a peak.

An empty corridor is not automatically respite. If it contains no decision, revelation, anticipation, or useful change of rhythm, it may simply be dead time.

An easy fight can be excellent respite when it lets the player demonstrate mastery, test a new technique safely, or enjoy a power gain. It fails when it is merely early-game filler copied into the late game.

### Difficulty tier and pacing role are independent

Pacing role should not be tied to a low or high threat tier.

- A late **opening** beat may be dangerous but conceptually clean: one new question, clearly presented.
- A late **respite** may contain meaningful choices, a familiar but still credible threat, or optional danger.
- An early **climax** can be locally intense without resembling the final boss.
- A **development** encounter can be harder than a later contrast beat if its role is to deepen an established problem.

Every stage of the game therefore needs legal opening, development, contrast, pressure, climax, and respite material appropriate to its current power and knowledge level. A pacing deck that exhausts these roles at higher tiers will collapse into repetition or inappropriate filler.

## 5. Reusable journey patterns

### 5.1 Protect the resolution after a peak

Once the climax is functionally over, the game should stop generating residue that prevents the player from feeling that it ended. A surviving nuisance, delayed projectile, long pursuit, or immediate new pull can consume the intended relief.

The journey needs a **peak fade**: a short period in which remaining pressure resolves and no new demand competes with the completion of the crisis.

This does not require a literal safe room after every hard fight. It requires enough separation for the player's state and attention to settle before the next escalation begins.

### 5.2 Raise the ceiling, not the entire floor

As the journey advances, peaks should become more demanding and the language should grow richer. Valleys must remain valleys relative to those peaks.

Late-game relief should not be identical to early-game relief, but it still needs lower immediate pressure, fewer simultaneous unknowns, or greater player control. If every late beat is held at maximum intensity, escalation becomes impossible and exhaustion replaces tension.

### 5.3 Introduce, develop, twist, conclude

A strong enemy, formation, terrain rule, or resource pressure usually benefits from four distinct uses:

1. **Introduce:** present the new idea clearly enough to read.
2. **Develop:** combine it with familiar material or change its geometry.
3. **Twist:** invert an assumption or pair it with a conflicting demand.
4. **Conclude:** give the player an encounter that proves understanding.

The four uses need not be consecutive or equally large. Their purpose is to turn content into learned language rather than one-off surprise.

### 5.4 Alternate the kind of work

Useful contrast includes:

- melee pressure followed by route choice;
- ranged crossfire followed by close-quarters commitment;
- attrition followed by resource allocation;
- novel enemies followed by a familiar mastery encounter;
- open terrain followed by constrained terrain;
- required danger followed by optional danger;
- fighting for survival followed by fighting for a reward;
- exploration followed by a decisive authored confrontation.

Changing enemy sprites while preserving the same decisions is not real contrast.

### 5.5 Foreshadow, test, and pay off

A later peak becomes satisfying when the journey has prepared its meaning.

- **Foreshadow:** show a landmark, aftermath, distant enemy, locked route, or incomplete version of the coming problem.
- **Test:** let the player interact with a limited or safer form of it.
- **Pay off:** require the player to combine the acquired knowledge, build, and resource judgment under pressure.

Foreshadowing creates anticipation; testing creates comprehension; payoff creates earned achievement.

### 5.6 Combine fixed anchors with variable connective tissue

Pure randomness cannot reliably create introductions, culmination, or memorable structural turns. Pure scripting weakens replayability and discovery.

Waystone should combine:

- authored anchor beats for introductions, major discoveries, bosses, distinctive locale moments, and necessary structural turns;
- bounded procedural sequencing for the encounters, routes, rewards, and variations connecting them.

The generator should vary a grammar while preserving the journey's essential punctuation.

### 5.7 Give the player optional intensity valves

Optional danger lets players partly regulate their own waveform. Examples include:

- a guarded cache;
- an elite visible off the main route;
- a risky shortcut;
- an uncertain side passage;
- a teacher, rare resource, or build opportunity behind a stronger threat;
- continuing while depleted instead of taking a safer detour or retreating.

An intensity valve works only when the risk and possible value are legible enough to support judgment. A concealed punishment is not a meaningful choice.

### 5.8 End beats by changing state

A situation should often leave at least one lasting consequence:

- resources changed;
- a route opened or closed;
- a shortcut was established;
- the build changed;
- a landmark was understood;
- a threat was removed or transformed;
- new information was acquired;
- an optional opportunity was accepted or forfeited;
- a new question was raised.

Without changes of state, even individually competent encounters can blur into a procession.

## 6. Expedition application

Expedition is forward-directed and default-permadeath. It can therefore support a comparatively strong authored macro-waveform. The generator's purpose is not to decide pacing freely; it is to choose varied legal expressions of the pacing role currently needed.

### 6.1 A local Expedition grammar

A useful locale grammar is:

**Arrival or preview → clean test → choice or contrast → twist → local peak → consequence**

Not every locale needs every step. A sparse area may omit a conventional fight. A gauntlet may deliberately compress release. A strange event may replace a tactical twist. Across the full run, however, the player should experience orientation, escalation, contrast, resolution, and consequence repeatedly.

### 6.2 Possible seven-locale macro roles

The following is a starting structure, not a fixed biome order:

1. **Orientation:** establish the run, current build, and primary language.
2. **Development:** deepen familiar problems and create the first meaningful build direction.
3. **Temptation:** emphasise optional risk, reward, and route commitment.
4. **Disruption:** introduce a substantial new rule, roster element, or structural surprise.
5. **Convergence:** combine earlier languages under increased pressure.
6. **Preparation or uneasy relief:** offer recovery, commitment, mastery, and anticipation without becoming empty.
7. **Approach and culmination:** frame, foreshadow, and deliver the boss.

The locale identity and the pacing role are separate layers. Different biomes can occupy these positions as long as they can express the required role.

### 6.3 Illustrative situation mix

For an Expedition of roughly 28 consequential situations, an initial test deck might contain:

- 10 standard or development combat situations;
- 4 major-pressure or set-piece situations;
- 4 light combat, mastery, or harassment situations;
- 6 discovery, route, reward, supply, or build situations;
- 3 disruptive, rare, or unusual situations;
- 1 boss.

These categories may overlap, but each situation should have one primary pacing role. These counts are an experimental starting point, not a balance recommendation. Complete-run testing should determine whether the journey has enough pressure, contrast, growth, and consequence.

### 6.4 Expedition sequencing constraints to test

- Do not place adjacent major peaks unless the segment is deliberately authored as a gauntlet.
- After a major peak, guarantee a resolving beat before the next escalation.
- Avoid more than two consecutive situations with the same primary problem or player verb.
- Provide a lasting reward, discovery, build change, or route consequence every few situations.
- Give every locale at least one contrast beat and one identity-defining beat.
- Use late-run opening and respite content appropriate to late-run power; do not recycle irrelevant early filler.
- Make the boss approach quieter than the boss so anticipation has room to form.
- Prevent droughts of healing, build development, discovery, or route choice without guaranteeing identical placement.

### 6.5 Adaptive pacing without invisible mercy

Expedition may respond to run state, but it should preserve consequence and determinism. Appropriate mechanisms include:

- guaranteed but variably located supplies;
- anti-drought rules;
- selection among legal beats of the required pacing role;
- optional rather than compulsory danger;
- deterministic conditions based on seed and visible run state;
- limits on repetition and recent-history weighting.

The generator should not secretly weaken an authored enemy because the player is hurt. It should manage upcoming opportunities and legal pacing roles, not falsify the combat rules.

## 7. Campaign application

Campaign cannot rely on a single ordered deck. Its journey is spatial, persistent, and partly controlled by the player. Pacing must emerge primarily from topology, distance, branches, checkpoints, shortcuts, discoveries, and coherent world-state changes.

### 7.1 The Waystone outward arc

A strong Campaign segment can follow this spatial rhythm:

**Safe anchor → familiar near field → uncertain branch → mounting commitment → outer ordeal → discovery or shortcut → rewarded return**

The outward journey increases uncertainty and the cost of failure. The shortcut is both a practical reward and the resolution of accumulated pressure. The return through newly connected or newly mastered space demonstrates progress.

### 7.2 Campaign journey principles

- Space near a Waystone should generally be cognitively lighter than the outer edge of its territory.
- Pressure should tend to rise with distance, uncertainty, and commitment, though not as a strict level gradient.
- Different branches should offer distinct intensity profiles rather than equivalent corridors with different scenery.
- A newly opened shortcut should remain meaningfully safer or more efficient long enough to feel like a reward.
- Return journeys should demonstrate mastery, changed access, or changed understanding rather than replaying the full climax.
- Revisits should combine a stable familiar base with occasional meaningful change; they should not feel as if the world rerolls itself without cause.
- Waystones should provide genuine decompression, while resting may coherently restore or respawn threats and therefore remains a consequential choice.
- Boss approaches should protect anticipation instead of exhausting the player with an unrelated peak immediately beforehand.
- NPCs, landmarks, mysteries, traversal, and route decisions should carry many of the valleys so that low combat does not mean low meaning.
- Persistent enemy deaths, discoveries, and shortcuts should let the player feel that difficult journeys alter the world.

### 7.3 Preserve world coherence

Campaign should not use overt adaptive spawn mercy that makes the world feel unstable or dishonest. Its pacing controls should primarily be authored into:

- the world graph;
- sightlines and approach routes;
- branch difficulty and optionality;
- shortcut placement;
- Waystone spacing;
- enemy state and respawn rules;
- conditional but intelligible changes to the world;
- resource and NPC placement.

The player should be able to learn the world's structure and use that knowledge deliberately.

## 8. Evaluation and telemetry

Individual encounter ratings cannot establish whether the journey works. Evaluation must include complete locale arcs and complete runs.

Useful measures include:

- consecutive situations with the same primary demand;
- meaningful replans or commitments per encounter;
- peak duration before genuine resolution;
- number of consequential decisions between peaks;
- health, potion, Energy/recovery, and consumable changes across a local arc;
- travel distance or world time without a decision, discovery, or meaningful anticipation;
- situations since the last lasting reward, route discovery, or build change;
- how often optional danger is accepted or declined, and why;
- whether players push onward, retreat, rest, or seek supplies at intended decision points;
- which situations players remember after completing a locale;
- whether low-intensity beats are rated satisfying, empty, or trivial;
- whether the boss approach produces anticipation or exhaustion;
- whether the resolving beat after a peak actually feels safe enough for the victory to register.

Because time freezes for the player, real-world duration is a weak measure by itself. Prefer:

- action commitments;
- movement commitments;
- changes of plan;
- resource decisions;
- revealed information;
- changes of state;
- authoritative world time.

### Questions for playtests

1. Where did pressure begin to build?
2. Did the last peak end cleanly, or did residue consume the relief?
3. What changed between one demanding situation and the next?
4. Did a low-intensity beat still give the player something worthwhile to do, learn, or anticipate?
5. Was the next peak foreshadowed and earned?
6. Did the player exercise meaningful control over risk, or merely follow the only viable route?
7. Did the locale leave a memorable tactical or spatial story?
8. Did late-game valleys remain genuine valleys without becoming irrelevant filler?
9. Did the journey change the player's resources, build, knowledge, access, or relationship to the world?

---

## 9. Failure modes

Journey design is failing when:

- every encounter is tuned as a climax;
- difficulty rises as a smooth numerical ramp with no real valleys;
- respite is only empty travel;
- a peak technically ends but unresolved enemies or immediate new pressure erase its resolution;
- successive encounters ask for the same answer despite different presentation;
- new mechanics appear once as surprises and are never developed or concluded;
- the generator is allowed to erase structural introductions, contrasts, or payoffs;
- late-game pacing lacks credible opening and respite material;
- adaptive pacing secretly changes combat rules or invalidates player knowledge;
- Campaign spaces reroll so aggressively that routes cannot be learned;
- Expedition randomness produces no run-wide story;
- bosses follow another unrelated maximum-intensity beat with no protected approach;
- situations leave no lasting consequence and blur into a procession;
- real-world minutes are used as the primary pacing measure despite frozen time.

---

## 10. Current design direction

The working direction for Waystone is:

### Expedition

Use an authored macro-waveform, bounded procedural sequencing, protected resolution after peaks, guaranteed contrast and resource beats, optional intensity valves, and late-tier content for every pacing role.

The run-wide structure is three biomes, each culminating in a boss, with a resource
refresh between biomes. This Hades-inspired rhythm gives each stage an introduction,
development, climax, and restorative transition. The earlier seven-locale examples
in this document illustrate pacing roles; they are not the current route contract.
See [Expedition design principles](expedition-design-principles.md#hades-legibility-role-combinations-and-expedition-rhythm).

### Campaign

Use spatial intensity gradients, differentiated branches, Waystone-centred outward arcs, persistent consequences, mastery on return, and shortcuts as both structural reward and emotional resolution.

### Shared standard

Do not ask only whether an encounter is excellent in isolation. Ask:

> What function does this situation perform here, what state does it change, and how does it prepare or resolve the beats around it?

The goal is a journey made from excellent beats—not a nonstop sequence of climaxes.

## Comparative foundations

These principles are consistent with several documented approaches from other games:

- Valve's *Left 4 Dead* Director formalised a cycle of build-up, sustained peak, peak fade, and relaxation. Its addition of a distinct peak-fade phase is especially relevant: combat must actually finish before relaxation can work. Major boss events remained authored outside ordinary adaptive selection because missing them would damage the campaign's structure. See Mike Booth, [*The AI Systems of Left 4 Dead*](https://steamcdn-a.akamaihd.net/apps/valve/2009/ai_systems_of_l4d_mike_booth.pdf).
- Nintendo designers have described a reusable four-part structure of beginning, development, twist, and conclusion. Its value for Waystone is not literal four-room repetition, but the transformation of a simple learned idea into a developed and concluded sequence. See [The Washington Post's interviews with Nintendo's Mario designers](https://www.washingtonpost.com/video-games/2020/09/14/mario-nintendo-creators-miyamoto-koizumi-tezuka-motokura/).
- Hidetaka Miyazaki described *Dark Souls* in terms of achievement and the surprise of discovery, with difficulty serving those goals. Its connected routes, bonfires, wrong turns, discoveries, and shortcuts show how a persistent world can create and resolve pressure spatially. See Game Informer's [*Afterwords: Dark Souls*](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2011/11/12/afterwords-dark-souls.aspx).
- David Pittman's account of *Eldritch* procedural level design illustrates the value of prescribed feature rooms, coherent themes, landmarks, and rare authored “hero” spaces embedded in generated connective tissue. See [*Procedural Level Design in Eldritch*](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2015/presentations/Pittman_David_Procedural%20Level%20Design.pdf).

These examples support the structural principles above; they do not prescribe Waystone's exact encounter counts, timings, or intensity ratios. Those remain hypotheses to test in Waystone's own frozen-time combat and journey structure.
